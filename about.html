About this playground

These are the interactive companions to the REII 211 course material. The written notes live in the course GitBook and get read top to bottom; this is the other half — a set of small tools that get poked at. They were split apart because the two have very different jobs.

How to use them

Every page has the same three regions. The rail on the left sets the problem up: how much data, in what order, which algorithm. The stage in the middle is the state of that problem right now. The workbench on the right walks it — Play runs the whole thing, Prev and Next move one step, and the counts at the bottom are what the algorithm has spent so far. Either side panel folds away with the two buttons at the right of the ribbon when you want the drawing bigger.

At the foot of the rail, Open or Save writes the problem the page is set up on to a .reii file — the array, the graph you drew, the maze, the offers — and opens it again later. A file opens on every page that can use it: save a shuffled array from one sorting page and open it on another, and the two comparison counts are finally about the algorithms rather than about two different arrays.

What they are not

They build intuition; they do not prove anything. A visualisation of one input tells you about that input. Small inputs show more than large ones, and changing a single parameter at a time is the fastest way to see what an algorithm is actually doing — run the same sort on a shuffled array and then on a reversed one and watch the counts.

Under the hood

Static, dependency-free and with no build step: clone it and open index.html, or serve the folder with python3 -m http.server. Every algorithm is written as a generator that yields one narrated step at a time and touches nothing else — no canvas, no timer, no colours — which is why the same code drives both the animation and the step buttons, and why each one can be checked on its own in the self-checks.

Acknowledgements

Built for the School of Electrical, Electronic and Computer Engineering at the North-West University. The scheduling page follows the interval-scheduling treatment in Skiena's The Algorithm Design Manual; the sorts and the graph algorithms are the standard ones from the course notes.
